IoC container and dependency injection

Inversion of Control: your classes stop creating their own collaborators. The container creates every bean and injects what each one needs.

In the franchise

Without a manager, the chef would have to hire his own storekeeper, and if the storekeeper changes, the chef's job description changes too (tight coupling). With a manager, the chef just says "I need a storekeeper". The manager hires everyone, introduces them, and replaces anyone when needed, even with a stand-in for training day (a mock in tests).

Three ways to inject

recommended
Constructor

Dependencies are final and mandatory, the object is never half-built, cycles fail fast, and tests just call new.

optional deps
Setter

For truly optional or re-configurable dependencies. The object can exist without them.

avoid
Field (@Autowired on a field)

Hidden dependencies, can't be final, needs reflection or Spring to test. Common in old code and tutorials.

Circular dependencies

A needs B and B needs A. Since Boot 2.6 circular references are prohibited by default, and with constructor injection they fail at startup with "The dependencies of some of the beans form a cycle". The right fix is a redesign: extract the shared logic into a third bean, or use events. @Lazy on one constructor parameter is a workaround, not a fix.

Interview questions

IoC vs DI?

IoC is the principle: control over creating and wiring objects moves from your code to a framework. DI is the technique Spring uses to implement it: dependencies are passed in (constructor, setter or field) instead of looked up or created.

Why is constructor injection preferred?

Immutability (final fields), required dependencies are obvious and guaranteed, no partially constructed objects, easy unit testing without a container, and circular dependencies surface immediately. Too many constructor parameters is a useful smell that the class does too much.
