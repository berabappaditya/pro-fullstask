Dependency Inversion Principle

High-level policy should not depend on low-level details. Both should depend on abstractions. Plug into a socket, don't wire into the wall.

At Ramu's chai stall

The flagship stall's stove was welded straight onto one company's gas pipe. When that supplier tripled prices, switching meant breaking the wall. So Ramu had the electrician fit a standard socket. Now gas stove, induction plate or electric kettle, anything with the standard plug works. The kitchen (high-level) depends on the socket standard (abstraction), and so does every appliance (low-level). Neither depends directly on the other.

Definition

1. High-level modules should not depend on low-level modules; both should depend on abstractions. 2. Abstractions should not depend on details; details should depend on abstractions. The abstraction is owned by the high-level side, which is why the dependency arrow is "inverted".

DIP, DI and IoC are three different things

TermWhat it isExample
Dependency Inversion PrincipleA design principle: depend on abstractions, with the abstraction owned by the high-level moduleOrderService depends on OrderRepository interface
Dependency InjectionA technique: give an object its dependencies from outside (constructor, setter, field)new OrderService(repo, notifier)
Inversion of ControlA broader idea: the framework controls object creation and the flow, calling your codeSpring's ApplicationContext creates beans and wires them

Prefer constructor injection: dependencies are explicit, fields can be final, objects are never half-built, and tests can use plain new. Field injection with @Autowired hides dependencies and needs reflection in tests.

Lock it in

Plug into a socket, don't weld to the pipe.

Business code depends on interfaces it owns; databases, email, HTTP clients are plugins. If you see new SomeConcreteService() inside business logic, you've found a DIP violation.

Interview questions

Why is it called "inversion"?

In a naive layered design, the source-code dependency follows the call direction: business → database. With DIP, the business layer defines the interface and the database layer implements it, so the source-code dependency points from the database layer up to the business layer, opposite to the flow of control. The dependency has been inverted.

With two implementations of an interface, how does Spring choose one?

It fails with NoUniqueBeanDefinitionException unless you disambiguate: mark one @Primary, use @Qualifier("sms") at the injection point, inject List<Notifier> or Map<String, Notifier> to get all, or activate one with @Profile / @ConditionalOnProperty.
