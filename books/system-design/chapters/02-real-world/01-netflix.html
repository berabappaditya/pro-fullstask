How Netflix works

Netflix splits itself in two: a "brain" in the AWS cloud that decides what you can watch and from where, and its own delivery network, placed inside internet providers, that sends the actual video bytes.

At Ramu's chai stall

Ramu now sells bottled chai all over India. If every bottle had to travel from his Medinipur factory when someone ordered it, deliveries to Chennai would take two days. So every night, while the roads are empty, his trucks pre-stock small fridges inside local kirana shops with the flavours each neighbourhood usually buys. When you order, the app (run from Ramu's head office) checks your account, takes payment, and tells you which nearby shop's fridge to collect from. Head office never touches the bottle itself.

Head office is Netflix's control plane on AWS. The fridges in kirana shops are Open Connect Appliances sitting inside your internet provider. Night-time restocking is the fill window.

The big picture: two planes

In plain words

Netflix is two companies glued together: a software company that runs a giant website/app in the cloud, and a "postal service" for video that has put its own post-office boxes inside your internet provider's building. The app decides; the box delivers.

What happens when you press Play

  1. RequestYour app calls the playback API through the Zuul gateway with the title, your device type and its capabilities (codecs, resolution, HDR, DRM type).
  2. ChecksThe playback service confirms your plan allows it (entitlement: number of screens, 4K or not) and prepares a DRM licence so only your device can decrypt the video.
  3. SteeringThe steering service looks at your IP and network, finds which Open Connect appliances near you hold this title in the formats your device supports, checks their health and load, and returns a ranked list of URLs.
  4. StreamThe app downloads the video in small chunks (a few seconds each) straight from the best OCA, choosing a quality for each chunk based on your current bandwidth (adaptive bitrate, below). If one OCA is slow, it switches to the next on the list.
  5. ReportWhile you watch, the app sends events (playing, buffering, quality changes, position) back to the cloud via Kafka-based pipelines. That powers "Continue watching", quality monitoring and recommendations.

Getting the video ready: the encoding pipeline

This is a classic batch, embarrassingly parallel job: split the work into independent pieces, process thousands at once, then stitch. It's the same idea as MapReduce, applied to video.

Adaptive bitrate: why the picture gets blurry instead of freezing

The app keeps a buffer of a few seconds of video ahead of what you're watching. For every next chunk it decides which quality to download. If your bandwidth drops (someone starts a big download, your train enters a tunnel), it switches to a lower quality before the buffer runs dry. A slightly blurry picture is better than a spinning wheel.

The cloud side: built to survive failure

gateway
Zuul

Every API call enters here: authentication, routing to the right service, rate limiting, canary routing of a slice of traffic to new versions. ({{ref:ch-micro}})

discovery
Eureka

Services register themselves and find each other by name, not IP, so instances can come and go freely.

resilience
Hystrix → Resilience4j

Netflix popularised circuit breakers and fallbacks: if recommendations fail, show a generic "Trending" row instead of an error page. ({{ref:ch-reliability}})

cache
EVCache

A Memcached-based, replicated cache in front of almost everything; most reads never touch a database. ({{ref:ch-cache}})

data
Cassandra

Viewing history and other huge, write-heavy, always-available data. An AP choice: a few seconds of staleness in "Continue watching" is fine. ({{ref:ch-cap}})

events
Kafka pipelines

Trillions of events (plays, pauses, errors) stream to analytics and recommendation systems. ({{ref:ch-queue}})

testing
Chaos engineering

Chaos Monkey randomly kills production instances during work hours, so teams must build services that shrug it off. Bigger tools fail entire regions on purpose.

regions
Active-active regions

Several AWS regions serve traffic at once; if one degrades, traffic is shifted to the others within minutes.

Recommendations: the reason you keep watching

Most of what members watch is found through recommendations. Heavy models train offline (batch jobs over viewing history), nearline jobs update your profile minutes after you watch something, and an online layer assembles your home page in milliseconds from precomputed results in EVCache. Even the artwork shown for a title is personalised: a romance fan and an action fan may see different thumbnails for the same film.

Real-world lesson: live is different

Netflix's whole delivery model is built on predicting demand and pre-positioning files overnight. Live events break that model: the content doesn't exist until the second it happens, and everyone wants the same seconds at once. During the Jake Paul vs Mike Tyson fight in November 2024 (around 65 million concurrent streams), many viewers saw buffering and errors. Live needs the techniques from the JioHotstar chapter ({{ref:ch-hotstar}}): massive pre-scaling, short cacheable segments and graceful degradation.

Lock it in

The brain is in the cloud; the bytes are in the neighbourhood.

Control plane (AWS): login, browse, recommend, decide which server. Data plane (Open Connect): video chunks from inside your ISP, filled overnight. The player adapts quality chunk by chunk so the video keeps playing.

Interview questions

"Design Netflix." Where do you start?

Clarify scope (browse + play, not billing). Split into control plane and data plane immediately: video bandwidth dwarfs everything else, so the core decision is a CDN strategy (pre-positioned caches near users), encoding into an ABR ladder, and a playback API that returns signed URLs to the best nearby servers. Then cover metadata (titles in a cached DB), user data (watch history in Cassandra), recommendations (offline + online), and resilience (fallbacks, multi-region).

Why doesn't Netflix just use a commercial CDN?

At its volume, owning the delivery network is cheaper and gives control over hardware, placement and software. Placing appliances inside ISPs also helps the ISPs (less traffic over their expensive upstream links), so they host the boxes for free. Smaller companies should simply use CloudFront, Akamai or Cloudflare.

How would you store "Continue watching" for 300M users?

A wide-column store like Cassandra partitioned by profile ID, with rows per title holding the last position and timestamp. Writes come every few seconds during playback, so it must be write-optimised and highly available; eventual consistency across regions is acceptable. Cache the top rows of each profile's home page.
