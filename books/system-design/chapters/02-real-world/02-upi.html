How UPI works and scales

UPI is a central switch (run by NPCI) that every bank and every payments app plugs into. A simple address like ramu@okaxis hides the bank account, and a carefully designed message flow moves money in seconds.

At Ramu's chai stall

Imagine every customer's bank needing a private phone line to every shop's bank before money could move. With 600+ banks, that's hundreds of thousands of lines, and each bank must trust and test every other bank. Instead, India built one telephone exchange that every bank calls. A customer scans the QR code on Ramu's counter, which says only ramu@okaxis. The exchange looks up which bank and account that is, asks the customer's bank to take ₹15 out, asks Ramu's bank to put ₹15 in, and tells both sides it's done. Ramu's soundbox says "₹15 received" a couple of seconds later.

The exchange is the NPCI UPI switch. ramu@okaxis is a VPA (Virtual Payment Address, now usually called a UPI ID).

The cast

you
Payer and payer's app

PhonePe, Google Pay, Paytm, BHIM or your bank's app. A third-party app works through a "sponsor" PSP bank.

PSP
Payment Service Provider bank

The bank that connects an app to UPI, issues UPI IDs (@okaxis, @ybl) and talks to NPCI on the app's behalf.

switch
NPCI UPI switch

The central hub: routes every message, resolves addresses, enforces rules and timeouts, and keeps the master record of each transaction.

remitter
Payer's bank (issuer)

Holds the payer's account, verifies the UPI PIN, and debits the money.

beneficiary
Payee's bank (acquirer)

Holds the receiver's account and credits the money.

settlement
RBI settlement

Banks don't move real money per transaction. NPCI nets everything and banks settle in batches through their RBI accounts several times a day.

Why a central switch: hub and spoke

In plain words

A UPI ID works like a website name. You type google.com, not an IP address; DNS finds the address. You pay ramu@okaxis, not an account number and IFSC; the switch finds the account. Ramu can change banks without reprinting his QR code if he moves the ID. This layer of indirection is one of the most useful tricks in system design ({{ref:ch-request}}).

Following one payment, step by step

LiveA ₹250 UPI payment, message by message
  1. StartPriya scans Ramu's QR (upi://pay?pa=ramu@okaxis&am=250) in her app. The app creates a unique transaction ID.
  2. Enter UPI PINThe PIN is typed into NPCI's secure library inside the app and encrypted with keys only the bank can use. Neither PhonePe nor Google Pay ever sees it.
  3. Resolve the addressThe payer's PSP sends the request to NPCI, which asks Ramu's PSP to turn ramu@okaxis into a real account.
  4. DebitNPCI sends a debit request to Priya's bank, which verifies the PIN and device and takes ₹250 out.
  5. CreditNPCI sends a credit request to Ramu's bank, which puts ₹250 in.
  6. ConfirmNPCI marks the transaction successful and notifies both sides. Priya sees the tick; Ramu's soundbox speaks.

When something goes wrong in the middle

The hardest case: Priya's bank debited the money, but Ramu's bank doesn't answer in time. Money has left one account and might or might not have reached the other. UPI's design handles it with patterns you know:

Unique transaction IDs

Every message carries the same ID and reference number, so retries and status checks are idempotent: asking twice never moves money twice. ({{ref:ch-api}})

Strict timeouts

NPCI caps how long each step may take. A hung bank doesn't hang the whole network. ({{ref:ch-reliability}})

"Pending" is a real state

The app shows "pending" instead of guessing. The transaction is a state machine: initiated → debited → credited / pending → success / reversed. ({{ref:ch-behavioral}})

Check status, then reverse

After a wait, the system asks for the final status; if the credit never happened, the debit is automatically reversed. That's a compensating action, exactly like a saga. ({{ref:ch-dtx}})

Real-world lesson: the retry storm of 2025

In April 2025, UPI suffered a major outage. NPCI found that some banks and payment apps were calling the "check transaction status" API far too often and at very high rates, so the switch's queues overflowed and genuine payments slowed down and failed. The fix, in a circular dated 21 May 2025 (compliance by 31 July 2025), was classic rate limiting ({{ref:ch-rate}}):

  • The first status check may only be sent 90 seconds after the original transaction.
  • Balance enquiries capped at 50 per app, per customer, per day; "list accounts" at 25.
  • Background jobs (autopay mandate execution, merchant list syncs) moved to non-peak hours; peak hours are 10 AM to 1 PM and 5 PM to 9:30 PM.
  • Members that don't comply risk API restrictions or a freeze on onboarding new customers.

The lesson: retries without backoff and limits turn a small slowdown into an outage.

How UPI stays secure

Device binding

On setup, the app sends an SMS from your SIM to prove the phone number is really in this device. Your UPI profile is tied to that device + SIM.

Two factors

Something you have (the bound device) + something you know (UPI PIN). Both are checked on every payment.

PIN never leaves encrypted

NPCI's common library encrypts the PIN on the phone; only the issuing bank can verify it. Apps and PSPs handle only ciphertext.

Limits and risk checks

Per-transaction and daily limits, cooling periods for new devices, and fraud scoring at banks and the switch.

How it scales to billions of transactions a month

Horizontal scale everywhere

App backends, PSP gateways and the switch itself are fleets of stateless nodes behind load balancers. ({{ref:ch-scaling}})

Active-active data centres

The switch runs across data centres at once, so losing one doesn't stop payments. ({{ref:ch-reliability}})

Asynchronous settlement

Real money between banks moves in net batches, not per payment: the fast path only updates ledgers. ({{ref:ch-queue}})

Tight response times

NPCI shortened its response-time limits for key APIs in 2025, so slow members fail fast instead of holding everyone up.

Protecting the hub

Per-member API rate limits and peak-hour rules stop one misbehaving bank or app from hurting everyone. ({{ref:ch-rate}})

Concentration limits

A proposed 30% cap on any one app's share of UPI volume (deferred to 31 December 2026) is about systemic risk: no single app should be a single point of failure for the country.

Lock it in

One exchange, a name instead of a number, and "pending" is an honest answer.

Hub and spoke for interoperability, VPA indirection like DNS, idempotent IDs + timeouts + reversal for in-flight failures, and rate limits with backoff so retries can't take the network down.

Interview questions

Design a UPI-like payment system. What are the critical properties?

Exactly-once money movement (idempotent transaction IDs, unique constraints), strong consistency for balances (CP: the debit and credit must reconcile), strict timeouts and an explicit pending state, automatic reversal for failed credits, a full audit trail, security (device binding, 2FA, encryption end to end), and protection of the central switch (rate limits, backpressure, active-active deployment). Settlement can be asynchronous and batched.

Money left my account but the payment shows "failed". What happened, technically?

The debit succeeded at the remitter bank, but the credit leg failed or timed out. The transaction is then reconciled: after status checks confirm the credit didn't happen, the switch triggers a reversal to the remitter bank. Until reconciliation completes, it can look "failed" in the app while the money is in transit back.

Why doesn't UPI just retry the credit forever?

Unbounded retries hold resources and can amplify load on an already struggling bank. A bounded number of checks with backoff, followed by a deterministic outcome (success or reversal) and end-of-day reconciliation, keeps the system stable and the customer informed.
