How JioHotstar survives the IPL rush

Live cricket creates the worst traffic shape in the internet: crores of people arriving within minutes, all wanting the same seconds of video, and leaving and returning together when a wicket falls. You can't react to that; you have to be ready before it happens.

At Ramu's chai stall

Ramu's stall is next to the stadium. On match days, nobody comes for hours, then 20,000 people arrive in the 15 minutes before the toss. When the home captain walks out to bat, the crowd doubles. When he's out, half of them rush out for chai at the same moment. If Ramu waits to see the crowd before lighting extra stoves, the stoves are still heating when the crowd is already angry.

So on match days he lights every stove an hour before the toss (pre-warming), keeps a timetable of when crowds come (scheduled scaling), rehearses with fake crowds (load testing), and if things still get out of hand he stops selling samosas and serves only chai (panic mode: drop everything except the core product).

The live pipeline

The traffic shape, and why autoscaling alone fails

Normal autoscaling watches CPU, notices it's high, and adds servers. That takes minutes: metrics need to be collected, new machines booted, apps started and warmed. Hotstar's engineers have described traffic growing by more than a million users a minute with roughly a 90-second window to react. By the time reactive scaling catches up, the spike has already hit.

Try all three strategies: reactive scaling always arrives late; pre-warming covers the known spikes; panic mode switches off non-essential features so each server can carry more viewers during surprises.

The playbook

before the match
Pre-warm and scale on a schedule

Match times are known. Capacity is raised in big steps well before the toss, instead of waiting for CPU alarms. Machine images are fully baked so new servers start fast.

during
Scale on concurrency, not CPU

The signal that predicts load is "how many people are watching and how fast that's growing". Scaling keys off that business metric, in steps (a "ladder").

rehearsal
Load test at full size

For 2019, the "Project Hulk" load tests used about 108,000 CPUs across 8 regions generating around 200 Gbps, including sudden "tsunami" surges and chaos experiments.

emergency
Panic mode: the video must play on

Every service is ranked. Under pressure, non-critical features (recommendations, personalisation, some widgets) are switched off so streaming, login and payments keep their capacity.

edge
Push APIs to the CDN

For 2023, frequently hit but cacheable APIs (scorecards, highlights) moved to a separate CDN domain with lighter rules, and refresh intervals were made longer, cutting origin calls.

clients
Polite clients

Apps retry with backoff and jitter, and push notifications go out in batches so they don't summon everyone in the same second. ({{ref:ch-reliability}})

Infrastructure lessons from the 2023 World Cup rebuild

Disney+ Hotstar's engineers have written about hitting walls that only appear at this scale. Each is a lesson about hidden single points of contention:

Wall they hitFixGeneral lesson
One NAT gateway per availability zone used 50% of its bandwidth at only 10% of expected loadOne NAT gateway per subnetFind shared chokepoints before the real event, with measurement
Several API gateway pods on one node saturated its network (8–9 Gbps)Bigger network nodes, and topology rules allowing one gateway pod per nodeSpread hot components; CPU isn't the only resource
Hundreds of per-service load balancers to pre-warm by hand; NodePort ports running outA shared Envoy-based gateway layer and direct pod routing (ClusterIP)Centralise cross-cutting concerns (routing, auth, rate limits, load shedding)
Adding 400+ nodes at once throttled the Kubernetes control planeStep-wise scaling, 100–300 nodes per stepEven the scaler has limits; scale in controlled steps
Latency-critical video services competing with heavy ads/personalisation workloadsSeparate clusters per workload type; one critical (P0) stack per cluster for headroomBulkheads at the infrastructure level ({{ref:ch-reliability}})
Lock it in

Light the stoves before the toss. Serve only chai when the crowd overflows.

Spikes faster than boot time need pre-warming and scheduled, step-wise scaling on concurrency. Rehearse with full-size load tests, push everything cacheable to the CDN, and keep a panic mode that sheds non-essential features so the video keeps playing.

Interview questions

Design a live-streaming platform for 5 crore concurrent viewers.

Video: ingest → live transcoding into an ABR ladder → packaging into short HLS/DASH segments → origin with a shield layer → multiple CDNs, with players fetching segments and adapting quality. APIs: stateless services on Kubernetes behind a gateway, cacheable responses on the CDN, sessions and entitlement in fast stores. Scale: pre-warm by schedule, scale on concurrency in steps, keep headroom, priority-based load shedding and feature kill switches. Prove it with full-size load and chaos tests. Clients: retries with jitter, batched notifications.

Why is scaling on CPU a poor fit for live events?

CPU is a lagging signal: it rises only after users arrive, and new capacity takes minutes to become ready, while viewers can arrive at over a million a minute. A leading signal (match schedule, concurrency and its growth rate) lets you scale before the load lands.

What would you switch off first in panic mode, and why?

Anything that isn't on the critical path to watching: personalised recommendations, social features, non-essential widgets, analytics with high fan-out. Keep login, entitlement, playback tokens, video delivery and payments. Decide this ranking before the event, wire kill switches into the services, and rehearse flipping them.
